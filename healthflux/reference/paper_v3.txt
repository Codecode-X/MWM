 1   Abstract

 2   Human health is a single underlying state that no measurement observes directly. Diagnoses,
 3   blood tests, molecular profiles and images capture different facets at different times. Inferring
 4   health from such evidence requires a representation that integrates every modality, persists be-
 5   tween observations and is revised as new evidence arrives. Here we introduce HealthFlux, a
 6   pan-modal world model that learns the latent dynamics of health from 4,920 features and three-
 7   dimensional brain and body MRI across eleven data domains in 502,166 UK Biobank partici-
 8   pants. Its hybrid state-space architecture combines ODE-based evolution between observations
 9   with continuous-time recurrent updates when new measurements arrive. In held-out partici-
10   pants, recursive simulations without further observations predicted 1,256 diseases and death
11   over twenty years, generally outperforming previously published state-of-the-art models. In
12   four independent US cohorts, its performance was comparable to or better than that of these
13   models. HealthFlux also exceeded specialized clinical risk scores for disease and mortality.
14   HealthFlux predicts diseases excluded entirely from training, correctly ranking a future case
15   above an unaffected person in 78% of comparisons, evidence that it has learned health itself
16   rather than the diseases it was trained on. Medication-conditioned virtual clinical trials sim-
17   ulated drug-associated physiological changes and downstream disease risk. Simulated effects
18   correlated with biomarker changes reported in 286 randomized trials and predicted disease and
19   safety effects in 228 randomized trials withheld from model fitting. Each modality contributes
20   information the others lack, and integrating them identifies individuals at risk whom single-
21   modality models miss. HealthFlux treats health itself as the object of prediction through one
22   continuously updated state, informed by any measurement, that supports disease-risk estimates
23   years before diagnosis.

28   diseases.4–7 The modelling problem is to infer a latent state that combines these partial obser-
29   vations, follows health between measurements and incorporates new evidence as it arrives.
30   Population-scale biobanks now bring these observations together in the same participants.8, 9
31   Biological-age measures capture differences in health among individuals of the same chrono-
32   logical age.10 DNA-methylation biomarkers predict morbidity and mortality,11–13 while longi-
33   tudinal molecular profiling and organ-specific analyses reveal distinct ageing trajectories across
34   individuals and physiological systems.14–16 These measurements differ in timing and coverage.
35   Blood tests may be repeated within months, MRI scans may be years apart, and diagnoses enter
36   the record when disease is clinically recognized. An individual’s physiological state must be
37   inferred from this incomplete and asynchronous evidence.
38   EHR models learn from sequences of clinical events,17–21 and multimodal approaches combine
39   biological and clinical inputs for disease prediction.22, 23 A general latent-state representation
40   must integrate information across these sources, retain it between observations and support pre-
41   dictions when disease-specific labels are scarce.
42   A world model represents a system through a latent state, simulates its dynamics and updates
43   the state when new observations arrive.24, 25 This corresponds to the task of inferring an in-
44   dividual’s changing health from partial clinical, molecular, physiological and anatomical ob-
45   servations. Continuous-time latent-state methods describe change between irregular observa-
46   tions,26–30 and medical world models learn patient trajectories from longitudinal records.31–33
47   Combining simulation between observations with observation-driven revision allows a health
48   representation to be queried between visits and updated as new information becomes available.
49   Here we introduce HealthFlux, a pan-modal world model that learns the latent dynamics of
50   health from clinical and biological observations in 502,166 UK Biobank participants. We test its
51   predictive value in held-out participants and in four US cohorts—the Coronary Artery Risk De-
52   velopment in Young Adults (CARDIA) study, the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC)
53   study, the Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) and the All of Us Research Program—and its gener-
54   ality through prediction of diseases excluded from training. Medication-conditioned state tran-
55   sitions are evaluated against observed biomarker responses and clinical-trial effects. Molecular
56   and anatomical analyses examine the biological information used by the model across disease
57   systems.

58   Results

59   Learning the latent dynamics of human health

60   HealthFlux integrated eleven data domains spanning longitudinal clinical records, demo-
61   graphics, genetics, blood biochemistry, haematology, physical function, lifestyle, proteomics,
62   metabolomics, body MRI and brain MRI. The model combined 4,877 time-dependent clinical-
63   event and numeric features, three-dimensional brain and body MRI volumes and 43 permanent-
64   context variables (Fig. 1a). Each MRI volume was encoded by a three-dimensional convo-
65   lutional encoder initialized from a pretrained MRI foundation model and fine-tuned jointly
66   with the world model. The architecture followed the way health is observed in practice (Fig.
67   1b). Clinical evidence is sparse and irregular: a blood test may be repeated within months, an
68   MRI scan may be acquired once, and a diagnosis is recorded only when disease is recognized,
69   whereas the underlying physiology changes continuously in between. HealthFlux therefore
70   carried each person’s health state forward in continuous time with a neural ordinary differential
71   equation conditioned on age and permanent context, so that the state could be read at any age,
72   including during long intervals without clinical contact. When a new diagnosis, measurement
73   or image was recorded, a stochastic gate weighed it against the state expected from the per-
74   son’s history, and a gated recurrent update revised the state, much as a clinician revises an
75   assessment after a new finding. The deterministic and stochastic components, with 256 and 128
76   dimensions, formed one 384-dimensional health state (Fig. 1c), from which shared decoders
77   estimated the risks of 1,256 diseases and death (Fig. 1d). An action branch, fitted with the
78   world model held fixed, represented a medication by its pharmacological properties and shifted
79   the health state after treatment started, so that futures with and without the medication could
80   be simulated for the same individual (Fig. 1b,e). Ablations supported both temporal pathways
81   (Figure S1). Removing the gated recurrent revision raised held-out next-event cross-entropy
82   from 4.66 to 5.06 and lowered the mean five-year AUROC across the 1,256 diseases by 0.041,
83   whereas removing ODE propagation left prediction after short intervals largely unchanged but
84   increased the next-event timing error after five or more observation-free years from 875 to 1,203
85   days. Across internal widths from 64 to 1,024 units, held-out next-event cross-entropy and
86   timing error were lowest at the width used in HealthFlux, and performance on the other metrics

88    We used the latent state to predict future disease and mortality in held-out UK Biobank partic-
89    ipants and evaluated disease prediction in four external cohorts (Fig. 1d). Disease prediction
90    was also tested after target diagnoses were excluded from world-model and readout training
91    (Fig. 1e,f). Separate experiments evaluated readouts on measurement identities or demographic
92    groups withheld during readout fitting (Fig. 3c,e). These tests examined whether a state learned
93    across conditions could support prediction with limited target-specific labels. Virtual clinical
94    trial simulations compared paired medication-on and medication-off trajectories initialized from
95    the same pre-medication state (Fig. 1e).
96    A separate Health State Index (HSI) summarized each state as a single number between 0 (death)
97    and 1 (the health of long-term disease-free participants), using a readout trained on survival and
98    disease burden. The same population-reference scale applied across participants and time points,
99    allowing individual trajectories to be compared.
100   Individual trajectories illustrate how the model integrates information over time (Fig. 1g). One
101   disease-free participant maintained a high HSI between ages 56 and 70. The participant who
102   developed multimorbidity at age 69.4 already had a lower HSI by age 55, with a further decline
103   after a vitreous-disorder record at about age 60.
104   Across held-out participants, the HSI at the baseline UK Biobank assessment declined with age
105   and stratified later outcomes (Figure S3). Each 1-SD lower HSI was associated with a 1.67-fold
106   higher hazard of death (95% CI 1.61–1.73) after adjustment for age and sex, and adding the HSI
107   to age and sex increased the 10-year concordance for death from 0.708 to 0.726. The 10-year
108   cumulative incidence of new multimorbidity fell from 71% in the lowest HSI decile to 11% in
109   the highest; between these deciles, incidence differed 13-fold for type 2 diabetes and 17-fold for
110   chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, but only 2-fold for cancer. Within each age band, the
111   lowest HSI quintile had two to three times the 10-year mortality of the other quintiles. The HSI
112   also agreed with participants’ own rating of their health, which the model did not receive: the
113   median HSI was 0.45 among participants who rated their health as excellent and 0.24 among
114   those who rated it as poor, an age- and sex-adjusted difference of 0.26 (95% CI 0.26–0.27).

122   Predicting disease from a persistent and revisable latent state

123   HealthFlux captured age-associated patterns of recorded disease incidence across respiratory,
124   reproductive, metabolic and cardiovascular conditions (Fig. 2a). Across 1,256 diseases and
125   death, it achieved a five-year mean AUROC of 0.832 and average precision of 0.032 in held-out
126   participants (Fig. 2b). The strongest evaluated comparator, RisQ,23 achieved a mean AUROC
127   of 0.805 and average precision of 0.021. HealthFlux also exceeded XGBoost,34 LightGBM,35
128   MILTON22 and Delphi-2M21 on the same endpoints and risk sets (Table 1). Figure 2c shows
129   disease-level five-year performance by disease system.
130   Observed disease sequences included type 2 diabetes followed by chronic kidney disease and
131   chronic ischaemic heart disease followed by heart failure (Fig. 2d). These sequences are con-
132   sistent with clinical descriptions of multimorbidity and disease progression.4–6
133   The inferred state responded to new clinical information. Participants with six or more new
134   diagnoses over 180 days had a 4.4-fold greater rate of state change than participants with no
135   new diagnoses (Fig. 2e). Endpoint-level agreement between predicted risk and observed inci-
136   dence across EHR-history strata provided a complementary cross-sectional check (Figure S5).
137   We simulated the latent state for one to five years while withholding all subsequent EHR events
138   and biological observations. At each query, the model predicted outcomes during the follow-
139   ing five years, so the last outcome window covered years 5–10 after the last observation. The
140   mean-AUROC advantage over Delphi-2M increased from 0.098 at a one-year gap to 0.128 at a
141   five-year gap (Fig. 2f). Participants with the highest predicted risks had greater observed disease
142   incidence. Across disease systems, mean five-year incidence enrichment ranged from 3.8-fold
143   to 6.8-fold in endpoint-specific top-10% groups and from 7.4-fold to 35.5-fold in top-1% groups
144   (Fig. 2g). For the median disease, the 10% of participants with the highest predicted risk ac-

223   Simulating the effects of medications on physiology and disease risk

224   We examined whether HealthFlux could simulate the effects of medications on physiology and
225   disease risk. Starting a medication was represented as an action that alters how the latent health
226   state evolves, and biomarker and disease-risk trajectories were compared between paired simu-
227   lations with and without the medication, both starting from the same pre-medication state. We
228   first considered statins and antihypertensive drugs, two classes with well-characterized clinical
229   effects. Following statin initiation, the medication-on trajectories showed lower LDL choles-
230   terol and apolipoprotein B, a more favourable health state and lower risks of chronic ischaemic
231   heart disease and stroke than the corresponding medication-off trajectories. Antihypertensive
232   initiation similarly lowered systolic and diastolic blood pressure, maintained a more favourable
233   health state and attenuated the accumulation of stroke and heart-failure risk. Observed biomarker
234   measurements were consistent with the medication-on trajectories (Fig. 4a).
235   To test whether medication effects learned from routine records agree with randomized evidence

238   We then emulated randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that reported biomarker changes relative
239   to placebo or a shared background therapy. Both arms of each trial were simulated from the same
240   baseline states of trial-eligible, held-out UK Biobank participants, and the simulated between-
241   arm difference was compared with the reported difference. Across 350 drug–biomarker pairs
242   from 286 RCTs, covering 96 drugs and 14 biomarkers, simulated and reported changes were
243   correlated (precision-weighted 𝑟 = 0.31, 95% CI 0.11–0.49), and the direction of change agreed
244   for 69% of the 124 pairs with a significant change in the trials (𝑃 < 0.05; Fig. 4b). Simulated
245   changes were smaller than reported changes, so this agreement reflected their relative ordering
246   rather than their absolute magnitude. Within UK Biobank, simulated changes also agreed with
247   the changes observed in held-out new users of 81 drugs (precision-weighted 𝑟 = 0.64); agree-
248   ment with trials was highest for lipids and blood pressure, and the results were robust to how
249   trial data were extracted (Figure S7).
250   Effects on disease are harder to learn from routine records, because people who start a medi-
251   cation often differ in health from those who do not (confounding by indication). We therefore
252   predicted the effect of a drug on each outcome by combining its HealthFlux drug and disease
253   representations with the trial design and the effects reported in other trials, and evaluated these
254   predictions on RCTs withheld entirely from model fitting. Across 655 drug–outcome compar-
255   isons from 228 RCTs comparing a drug with placebo or with no added drug, covering 98 drugs
256   and 79 outcomes that included target diseases, adverse events and death, predicted and observed
257   odds ratios were correlated (precision-weighted 𝑟 = 0.32, 95% CI 0.23–0.41). Among the 84
258   comparisons with a significant effect in the trial (𝑃 < 0.05), the direction was predicted correctly
259   for 76% of increases in risk and 71% of decreases (Fig. 4c). Observed effects increased with
260   predicted effects and lay close to the line of identity, both in cross-validation and in a sealed test
261   set of 46 RCTs that was evaluated once (Fig. 4d). In this test set, the correlation was 0.46 (95%
262   CI 0.24–0.63), compared with 0.12 (−0.06 to 0.34) for extrapolation from pooled estimates of
263   the same drug and outcome in other trials (difference 0.34, 95% CI 0.11–0.52). Of the twelve
264   outcomes with the strongest trial evidence, ten were predicted in the correct direction, including
265   more hypoglycaemia with glimepiride, more hypotension with candesartan and irbesartan, and
266   fewer deaths with simvastatin and bisoprolol (Fig. 4e).

352   Discussion

353   HealthFlux models human health as an evolving latent state inferred from clinical events, labo-
354   ratory and physical measurements, lifestyle, molecular profiles and imaging. It learns dynamics
355   that allow the state to be simulated between observations and revised as new evidence arrives,
356   and a single state supports risk estimates for 1,256 diseases and death.
357   In clinical terms, the main results can be stated as risks and proportions. For the median disease,
358   the 10% of held-out participants whom HealthFlux ranked at highest risk accounted for 51% of
359   new cases over the following five years and were 9.5 times as likely to develop the disease as
360   the remaining 90%. Among the 1% at highest predicted risk, 29% were diagnosed with heart
361   failure within five years, compared with 1.4% of all participants, and 74% died, compared with
362   4.2%. The model retained this ability without new information: simulated forward for twenty
363   years without further observations, it ranked a person who developed a disease above one who
364   did not in 84 of 100 random pairs, compared with 58 of 100 for Delphi-2M. At alert thresholds
365   that would flag 5% of people without the disease at a routine visit, it flagged a median of 57% of
366   future cases with extensive prior morbidity four months before diagnosis. In randomized trials
367   withheld from model fitting, it predicted the direction of 76% of significant increases and 71%
368   of significant decreases in disease and safety outcomes, and integrating modalities identified
369   28–56% of future hypertension cases missed by single-modality models.
370   Each diagnosis can also be placed within a longer model-inferred trajectory. The Health State
371   Index (HSI), a single score between 0 (death) and 1 (the health of long-term disease-free partic-
372   ipants) read from the health state, summarizes these trajectories on a common scale. Each 1-SD
373   lower HSI at baseline was associated with a 1.67-fold higher hazard of death after adjustment
374   for age and sex, and the 10-year incidence of new multimorbidity was 71% in the lowest HSI
375   decile and 11% in the highest. Two years before the first record of 29 of 31 chronic conditions,
376   future cases had a lower HSI than matched controls of the same sex and age (Figure S4). For
377   heart failure, the future case had the lower HSI in 70% of case–control pairs, and the mean HSI
378   of future cases was already 17% lower than that of controls ten years before diagnosis (0.31
379   versus 0.37). A diagnosis can therefore be placed within a process already under way, and fol-
380   lowing these trajectories allows gradual changes between observations to be examined alongside
381   changes accompanying newly recorded illness.

382   Learning across diseases is particularly useful when target-specific labels are scarce: for rare
383   diseases with too few recorded cases to train a dedicated model, for conditions that are newly
384   defined, such as post-COVID-19 condition, which had no dedicated diagnosis code before the
385   pandemic, and for health systems in which an outcome is seldom coded. HealthFlux predicted
386   disease risk after target diagnoses were removed from both world-model and readout training,
387   ranking a future case above a non-case in 78 of 100 pairs, close to the 83 of 100 achieved with
388   full training labels. This transfer indicates that the learned state contains predictive information
389   shared across diagnostic categories. The assay and demographic experiments reused the same
390   representation, with assay identities and group-specific disease labels withheld at the readout-
391   fitting stage, and the resulting readouts predicted repeat measurements and disease risk in these
392   held-out settings.
393   The molecular and imaging analyses gave biological content to this evolving state. Proteomic,
394   metabolomic, physiological and anatomical measurements contributed distinct disease signals,
395   and similar disease-response profiles connected measurements within and across modalities.
396   Subcortical structures most consistently raised the predicted risk of brain-related diseases.
397   Brain-MRI sensitivity aligned with anatomical differences preceding cardiometabolic diag-
398   noses, whereas type 2 diabetes prediction drew on information beyond body anatomy.
399   An evolving latent state brings measurements collected at different times into a common repre-
400   sentation for disease prediction. Our evaluations used diagnoses as recorded in health records,
401   which lag disease onset and depend on contact with health care, and prediction in CARDIA,
402   ARIC and WHI used adaptation to unlabelled pre-landmark histories. Prospective studies and
403   independent cohorts with repeated molecular measurements and imaging can test how these
404   dynamics relate to functional decline and the onset of disease, and whether updating the state
405   improves clinical decisions as new observations arrive.
