Our vision

We want to understand aging well enough to extend healthy human lifespan by an order of magnitude.

Evolution has already changed maximum lifespan more than a hundredfold. A mouse lives about 4 years and an ocean quahog more than 500, while the best interventions extend mouse lifespan by a small fraction. We think the differences between species depend on mechanisms that current interventions do not reach, and we use AI to find them.

The lab is computational and experimental, so the predictions we act on are tested in cells and animals.

Research

Aging shows itself in whole organisms, in populations and in the lifespan of a species, while most AI for biology works on proteins and cells. We model all six scales below and start much of our work at the larger ones.

AI agents that do research

Public repositories hold millions of molecular profiles, and most were analyzed once for the question of the study that produced them. Our agents read these data together with the literature, run the analyses and propose what to test. Each result keeps the data, code and papers it came from.

In ClockBase Agent the agents scored public mouse profiles with aging clocks and nominated interventions predicted to lower biological age. In the preprint, one of them, ouabain, slowed frailty progression and reduced neuroinflammation in aged mice.

Models that simulate biology

A model of a biological system should predict its future state and how it responds to an intervention. HealthFlux follows how a person's health state changes from clinical and molecular records, and predicting the response to an intervention is the next step. ESM-Life relates protein sequences to lifespan across species.

We also study aging from physical first principles, as the slow loss of the stability that repair maintains, and use that theory to decide what the models should predict.

Brain resilience

Both tracks start with the aging brain. Aging is the largest risk factor for Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, yet some people keep their memory into old age with the pathology in their brains, and we want to know what protects them.

We expect the longest-lived species to have the most resilient brains. A bowhead whale can live about two centuries and a naked mole-rat outlives other rodents of its size by decades, so we compare their brains with human brains to find protective molecules and cell states.

In people we model brain aging from molecular profiles of brain tissue, blood and spinal fluid. We define resilience as how far a person's brain stays ahead of what their age and pathology predict, and we treat whatever explains that difference as a target.

      People

      Kejun Albert Ying, PhD

      Principal investigator. Incoming Assistant Professor.

      Albert builds AI for biology and aging, from foundation and world models of biological data to agents that analyze public data and propose experiments. His earlier work includes causal aging clocks and MethylGPT.

      He received his PhD from Harvard University in 2025 with Vadim Gladyshev for the dissertation On the Quantification of Aging. He did his postdoctoral research with Tony Wyss-Coray and David Baker.

      He was an NIH/NIA F99/K00 fellow, received the 2026 WAIC Yunfan Award and is a core member of the Biomarkers of Aging Consortium.
